Haleakalā Sunrise & Beach
Book this one ahead. Haleakalā sunrise needs either a guided tour or a recreation.gov vehicle permit reserved in advance — see below. Don’t leave it to the morning of.
Pre-dawn
Haleakalā Sunrise (Suggested)
Summit
Sunrise from the 10,023-ft summit — above the clouds, near-freezing, unforgettable. Two ways to do it:

We recommend a guided tour. Less stress: no driving the dark summit road, the park entry permit is included, and breakfast usually comes with it. Book early — they fill up.

If you self-drive: you must reserve a vehicle sunrise permit on recreation.gov (per vehicle, not per person · $1 + $30 park entry). Reservations release 60 days ahead at 7:00 AM HST, with a smaller batch 48 hours ahead — both sell out within minutes. Create your recreation.gov account NOW (you must be logged in to grab one), and have a backup date ready.

Island Overview
Maui — The Valley Isle
Maui is shaped like two volcanoes fused at the middle — the older, eroded West Maui Mountains on one side and the massive Haleakalā shield volcano on the other, connected by a flat central isthmus. That geography creates six distinct regions with completely different personalities, microclimates, and reasons to visit. Maui is not one destination. It’s several, layered into one island.
The Six Regions
West Maui
Kā‘anapali · Kapalua · Lahaina
The original resort corridor. Kā‘anapali is reliably sunny with calm water and full resort infrastructure. Kapalua, just north, is quieter and more refined — the Ritz-Carlton sits on a ridge above bays with some of the best snorkeling on the island. Lahaina, the historic whaling town, was devastated by wildfire in August 2023. The waterfront district is being rebuilt. West Maui is still operating, but the cultural anchor has changed.
South Maui
Kihei · Wailea · Mākena
Where most luxury travelers end up — and for good reason. South Maui is dry, consistently sunny, and home to Wailea’s resort row: the Grand Wailea, Fairmont Kea Lani, Four Seasons, Andaz. Kihei to the north is more casual and condo-heavy. Mākena, south of Wailea, is where the development stops — Big Beach is one of the largest white sand beaches on the island. The most reliable choice for clients who prioritize beach weather and resort amenities.
Central Maui
Kahului · Wailuku
Nobody vacations here on purpose, but every traveler passes through. Kahului has the island’s main airport and big-box reality of island life. Wailuku, just inland, is the actual county seat — an old plantation town with antique shops and local diners. Iāo Valley is here: a narrow, mist-covered gorge with a dramatic rock needle rising from the jungle floor.
Upcountry Maui
Makawao · Kula · Hāli‘imaile · Haleakalā
Climb Haleakalā’s slopes and the temperature drops, the landscape opens into rolling pasture, protea farms, and eucalyptus groves. Makawao is a paniolo — Hawaiian cowboy — town with a rodeo history and indie galleries. Kula is farm country: lavender, strawberries, goat cheese. Haleakalā National Park is the primary reason visitors come up here — the summit crater at 10,023 feet is otherworldly. Sunrise reservations are required — book through recreation.gov well in advance.
North Shore
Pā‘ia · Ho‘okipa · Hāiku
Funky, windswept, and surf-obsessed. Pā‘ia is a small plantation town turned bohemian village — the last stop before the Road to Hana — with good food, surf shops, and an energy that feels nothing like Wailea. Ho‘okipa Beach is one of the premier windsurfing venues on earth. The North Shore gets more wind and rain than the south, but it has the most character.
East Maui — Road to Hana
Hāna · Kīpahulu · Oheo Gulch
The Road to Hana is 52 miles of narrow two-lane road through dense rainforest, crossing over 50 bridges. The drive itself is the experience — waterfalls appearing around blind corners, bamboo forests, pull-offs to black sand beaches. Hāna town is tiny, quiet, and unhurried. The Hotel Hāna-Maui is one of the most peaceful properties in the state. Clients who rush the Road to Hana and turn around the same day miss the point. Overnight in Hāna if the budget allows.
Insider Tips
Maui has more white sand beach than any other Hawaiian island — over 30 miles. But not every beach is swimmable. Conditions vary dramatically by season and location. Always check current conditions.
The Lahaina fire changed West Maui significantly. Historic Front Street is largely gone. Hotels in Kā‘anapali and Kapalua are operating normally, but set accurate expectations. The community is healing — tourism is welcome and needed.
Haleakalā sunrise requires a timed entry reservation booked through recreation.gov, often weeks in advance. They enforce it. Clients who don’t book ahead cannot get to the summit at sunrise.
Whale season runs November through May, peaking January through March. The channel between Maui, Lāna‘i, and Kaho‘olawe is one of the most important humpback breeding grounds in the North Pacific.
Molokini Crater is a partially submerged volcanic atoll off the South Maui coast — one of the top snorkeling sites in Hawaii. Visibility regularly hits 150 feet. Book morning trips; afternoon wind can make it choppy.
Trade winds blow from the northeast, making the windward sides wetter and the leeward sides drier. That’s why Wailea is almost always sunny and Hāna is almost always lush.
Upcountry is underrated. Most resort-focused itineraries skip it entirely. Clients who do one Upcountry day — Makawao lunch, Kula farm stop, Haleakalā sunset — often say it was the most memorable part of the trip.
Region Guide
West Maui
Kā‘anapali · Kapalua · Lahaina
West Maui begins where the highway curves around the back of the West Maui Mountains and the ocean suddenly appears — wide, blue, and calm on the leeward side. The resort strip runs north from Lahaina through Kā‘anapali and up into the cooler, quieter reaches of Kapalua. Post-fire, it’s still operating, still beautiful, and still where many of the island’s most iconic resort experiences live. But the cultural center — Lahaina — is in recovery, and the landscape of what visitors find there has changed.
Towns & Areas
Kapalua
Quiet refinement at the northern tip
The road thins out and the crowds thin with it. Kapalua is home to the Ritz-Carlton Maui and some of the island’s best snorkeling bays — Kapalua Bay is sheltered by lava points on either side, making it one of the calmest entries on the west coast. DT Fleming Beach to the north is broader and more open. Dragon’s Teeth, a jagged lava formation at Makaluapuna Point, is one of those Maui spots that looks almost fictional — wind-sculpted rock points rising from the edge of the ocean. Cooler than the south, breezier, less crowded.
Kā‘anapali
Classic resort Maui
Three miles of wide, white beach lined with major hotels — Hyatt, Marriott, Sheraton, Westin — and Whalers Village shopping center anchoring the middle. Highly walkable along the shore. Black Rock (Pu‘u Kekā‘ā) at the north end is the snorkel spot — protected, calm, full of fish and sea turtles. Every evening at sunset, a torch lighter performs a cliff dive from Black Rock — a tradition tied to the Sheraton Maui and free to watch from the public beach. Good for families, first-timers, and clients who want everything within walking distance.
Lahaina
The historic town being rebuilt
For 150 years, Lahaina was the cultural and commercial anchor of Maui — a former whaling capital with a waterfront lined with restaurants, galleries, and the famous Banyan tree planted in 1873 that grew to cover nearly an acre. The August 2023 wildfire destroyed most of the historic district. The Banyan tree survived and is recovering. The harbor still operates and some businesses have reopened. But advisors must be clear with clients: Lahaina today is not the Lahaina of pre-2023 travel guides. The community is actively rebuilding — tourism is welcome and needed, but set accurate expectations before arrival.
Olowalu
The quiet stretch between
A blink-and-miss-it stretch of coast between Lahaina and the isthmus. Olowalu has a shallow reef that produces consistent snorkeling with frequent sea turtle sightings — a dedicated stop for snorkel-focused clients who know to look for it. There’s a general store and not much else. Worth keeping in the back pocket for the right client.
Insider Tips
The Kahekili Highway (Hwy 340) loops around the back of the West Maui Mountains — largely empty and genuinely dramatic above the ocean cliffs — but narrow and rough in sections. Some rental car companies prohibit it in their agreements. Confirm rental terms before recommending it.
Dragon’s Teeth is beautiful and has no guardrails. The lava edge drops directly to ocean. Shoes are essential and children need to be actively managed back from the edge.
The cliff dive ceremony at Black Rock happens nightly at sunset at the Sheraton Maui. It’s open to watch from the public beach as a non-guest. Worth timing a Kā‘anapali evening walk around it.
Whale season in the Lāhainā Roads channel runs roughly November through May, peaking January through March. Lahaina Harbor whale watch departures are still operating post-fire.
West Maui gets windier in the afternoon. Trade winds accelerate around the northern tip by mid-afternoon. Morning hours are calmer for snorkeling at Kapalua Bay and Olowalu.
DT Fleming Beach is state-managed — no resort fee to access it. Broad, scenic, and far less crowded than Kā‘anapali. Surf can be significant in winter; better for walking than swimming in swell.

South Maui
Kihei · Wailea · Mākena
South Maui is the dry side — the leeward coast where the West Maui Mountains and Haleakalā block the trade winds and the rain clouds slide past. The sun here is almost a guarantee. The coast runs from Kīhei in the north down through Wailea’s resort row into Mākena, where the pavement eventually ends and lava fields take over. This is the region most international travelers picture when they imagine a Maui vacation: turquoise water, white sand, luxury hotels, consistent sunshine.
Towns & Areas
Kīhei
Casual, condo-heavy, and practical
Kīhei is the unpretentious counterpart to Wailea — condos instead of resorts, grocery stores instead of poolside service. Long stretches of beach, from Kamaole Beach Parks I through III, are wide and swimmable with county park facilities. Popular with repeat visitors, budget travelers, and anyone who wants to cook their own meals and live like a semi-local. Not glamorous, but genuinely useful for the right client profile.
Wailea
The resort core
This is where the flagship properties sit: the Four Seasons Maui at Wailea, the Grand Wailea (Waldorf Astoria), the Fairmont Kea Lani, and the Andaz Maui at Wailea Resort. The Wailea Beach Path connects them along a mile and a half of coastline. The Shops at Wailea anchor the center of the strip. For luxury clients, Wailea is the default South Shore answer, and the infrastructure consistently backs it up.
Mākena
Where Maui gets wild again
South of Wailea, the manicured landscaping gives way. Mākena’s Big Beach — officially Oneloa Beach — is one of the largest undeveloped white sand beaches in the state: a broad golden sweep with no hotels behind it and strong body-surf waves. The shore break can be powerful — injuries happen here regularly. Little Beach, over the lava point at the north end, is clothing-optional and known for its Sunday drum circle. South of Big Beach the road passes through lava field, and the sense of the island’s edge is real.
Insider Tips
All Hawaii beaches are public by law. Guests at Kīhei condos can walk the same Wailea shoreline as Four Seasons guests. The Wailea Beach Path and its access points cannot be restricted by resort properties.
Big Beach shore break is not always swimmable. The steep drop-off creates a hard, fast break that looks manageable until it isn’t. Brief clients to watch the waves for several minutes before entering and to pay attention to posted flag conditions.
Molokini Crater sits about three miles offshore from Mākena — a partially submerged volcanic crater and marine preserve with visibility regularly reaching 100 feet or more. Most tours depart from Mā‘ālaea Harbor. Book morning departures; afternoon trade winds create chop.
Keawakapu Beach, at the southern edge of Kīhei, is one of the most underused beaches on the island — wide, clean, and far less crowded than the Kamaole parks. Worth flagging for clients who want South Shore conditions without the resort foot traffic.
The Shops at Wailea close earlier than clients expect — typically by 9 PM. For evening dining, reservations are essential. Walk-in availability at Wailea resort restaurants is limited in peak season.
Mākena Road does not connect south. It ends at lava field. There is no shortcut to the airport from Mākena — clients who drive out expecting a loop will need to backtrack.

Central Maui
Kahului · Wailuku · ‘Īao Valley
Central Maui is the flat, hot isthmus between Maui’s two volcanic masses — the practical interior that makes the rest of the island function. Nobody puts it on a vision board, but every visitor passes through, and advisors who know it well can find several things genuinely worth stopping for.
Towns & Areas
Kahului
The working hub
Airport, Costco, Target. Kahului is where the island runs its logistics. The Maui Arts and Cultural Center hosts concerts and performances year-round. Queen Ka‘ahumanu Center covers most basic shopping needs. Advisors should know Kahului well enough to route clients through it efficiently — this is the refueling and reorienting stop, not the destination.
Wailuku
The real town
Just inland and uphill from Kahului, Wailuku is the county seat and one of the most authentic towns on the island. Market Street has locally owned restaurants, antique shops, and a working-town energy that has nothing to do with resort Maui. Takamiya Market is a long-standing local deli with a prepared foods counter that residents rely on. Sam Sato’s, a noodle shop known for dry mein and manju, has been a Wailuku institution for decades. This is the Maui that regulars discover and keep to themselves.
‘Īao Valley State Monument
The dramatic detour
A few miles up the road from Wailuku, the valley narrows fast and the walls go green and vertical. The ‘Īao Needle — a moss-covered rock spire rising roughly 1,200 feet above the valley floor — is the visual anchor. The air is cooler, the ferns are enormous, and the sound of the stream running below fills everything. A 20-minute stop that earns its place on any itinerary and one of the most dramatic natural features on the island.
Insider Tips
Go to ‘Īao Valley in the morning. The parking area fills on busy days by mid-morning, and afternoon rain frequently rolls into the valley. An early start gets you the mist, the green walls, and a parking spot.
‘Īao Valley requires a parking fee — confirm current rates and any reservation requirements before client arrival. Hawaii has been expanding its state park reservation systems and policies are subject to change.
Wailuku is the practical solution when clients need a pharmacy, specialty grocery, or a meal that isn’t priced for tourists. It’s also the fastest route to the ‘Īao Valley and Upcountry from the airport corridor.
The Maui Arts and Cultural Center in Kahului runs a genuine performance calendar — local acts, touring musicians, and cultural events. Checking the schedule for a client’s dates can add a local dimension no resort activity desk can replicate.
Highway 30 through the isthmus backs up during morning and afternoon commute hours between Kahului and Kīhei. Clients heading from the airport to a South Maui hotel in late afternoon should factor in potential delays.

Upcountry Maui
Makawao · Kula · Haleakalā
Turn away from the ocean, point toward Haleakalā, and start climbing. The temperature drops roughly three degrees per thousand feet of elevation. What were once sugarcane and pineapple fields give way to eucalyptus, then pasture, then protea farms with views down to both coasts simultaneously. Upcountry is the part of Maui most visitors miss entirely and most repeat visitors prioritize.
Towns & Areas
Makawao
The paniolo town that reinvented itself
Makawao was a Hawaiian cowboy town — paniolo culture, working ranches, rodeo — and some of that DNA is still visible in the wooden storefronts and at the annual Fourth of July Makawao Rodeo, one of the oldest in the state. Today it’s also indie galleries, good lunch spots, and Komoda Store and Bakery, which has been making cream puffs and stick donuts since 1916. Makawao is the Upcountry anchor — worth 90 minutes minimum for any client making the drive.
Hāli‘imaile
One restaurant, one distillery, one road
Hāli‘imaile is known for two things: Hāli‘imaile General Store, Beverly Gannon’s farm-to-table restaurant and a Maui institution for decades, and Hāli‘imaile Distilling Company, home of Pau Maui Vodka made from Hawaiian pineapple. A 10-minute detour off the main Upcountry road that pays off for food-focused travelers and anyone wanting a genuinely local lunch stop.
Kula
The farm belt
Kula is agricultural Maui. Ali‘i Kula Lavender Farm sits on a hillside with lavender in rows and sweeping views across the isthmus on clear days. Surfing Goat Dairy offers tours and cheese tastings. This is where Maui chefs source — strawberries, greens, herbs, and goat cheese available roadside at elevations that produce flavors the coast can’t match.
Haleakalā
The summit that reorients everything
At 10,023 feet, the summit sits above the clouds on most mornings. The landscape up top is mineral and silent: cinder cones in shades of rust and charcoal, silversword plants that grow nowhere else on earth, altitude and wind that remind you this is a serious environment. The drive from sea level to the summit takes roughly 90 minutes. Sunrise entry requires a timed reservation through recreation.gov, typically opening 60 days in advance — peak-date slots sell out within minutes of opening. Daytime and sunset visits do not currently require reservations.
Insider Tips
Komoda Store and Bakery sells out of cream puffs and stick donuts, often before 10 AM on busy days. Komoda is closed certain days of the week and does not restock. Go early or call ahead. This is not an exaggeration.
The silversword plant (‘āhinahina) is endemic to Haleakalā — it grows nowhere else in the world in this form, blooms once in its lifetime, produces seeds, and dies. Briefing clients on this before the summit changes how they experience the walk through the crater.
Haleakalā summit temperatures can be near freezing in any season — regularly in the 30s and 40s °F with wind chill, even when it’s 88°F at the beach. Layers, a jacket, and closed shoes are mandatory, not optional.
Altitude sickness is possible at 10,023 feet. Headaches, dizziness, and nausea are real. Recommend ascending slowly, staying hydrated, and avoiding alcohol the night before. Clients with heart or respiratory conditions should consult a physician.
Sunrise reservations through recreation.gov open at midnight Hawaii time, exactly 60 days before the visit date. For peak summer dates and holiday weeks, they disappear within minutes. Book at exactly 60 days out.
Upcountry temperature swings catch clients off guard. When South Maui is 88°F, Kula can be 65°F with a sweater wind. Clients planning a full Upcountry day should dress in removable layers even in summer.

North Shore
Pā‘ia · Ho‘okipa · Hāiku
The North Shore of Maui doesn’t feel like resort island. The wind picks up, the vegetation changes, and Pā‘ia appears at a curve in the road. The energy is different here — surf culture, organic food, art, and a pace that doesn’t organize itself around checkout times. The North Shore is Maui’s bohemian edge and the launching point for the Road to Hāna.
Towns & Areas
Pā‘ia
Bohemian heart of the North Shore
One intersection, a half-mile of pastel plantation storefronts, and more character per square foot than anywhere else on Maui. Pā‘ia began as an 1880s sugar-mill town; when the mill wound down and the hippies arrived in the ‘60s and ‘70s, they made it their unofficial capital — and somehow, against the odds of island real estate, it never got polished into a resort. The bohemian streak is still real, not staged. A slow walk down Baldwin Avenue can put you shoulder to shoulder with old-school hippies, pro windsurfers straight off Ho‘okipa, new-age mamas, the odd Hollywood actor, and a street-corner preacher — you may even pass a hand-lettered ‘Don’t Feed the Hippies’ sign or a kid busking outside the Bank of Hawai‘i. Mana Foods is the town’s true center of gravity: the best and cheapest natural-foods store on the island, and the social hub where the whole North Shore crosses paths. Charley’s, the saloon on the main drag, is local legend — Willie Nelson, a longtime Maui resident, has been known to turn up unannounced and sit in with the band. Practically, Pā‘ia is also your last real gas, grocery, and restroom stop before the Road to Hāna — and Mama’s Fish House, just east at Kū‘au Cove, remains the island’s most-requested reservation. Don’t just gas up and bolt — give it twenty minutes on foot.
Ho‘okipa Beach
Wind and water at their loudest
A short drive east of Pā‘ia, Ho‘okipa Beach Park is one of the premier windsurfing and kiteboarding venues in the world. On a trade wind afternoon, watching professional-caliber riders launching off open ocean swells from the cliff overlook is genuinely remarkable. The beach itself is not a swimming beach. Sea turtles haul out on the sand in the late afternoon — a reliable, well-documented phenomenon — creating a striking juxtaposition of reptilian stillness and athletic chaos in the surf above them.
Hāiku
The quiet residential interior
Turn off the coastal road and the jungle closes in. Hāiku is where long-stay visitors rent plantation cottages tucked into banana and bamboo. No resorts here — just community, lush landscape, and a Saturday farmers market. Not a sightseeing destination, but directly relevant for advisors whose clients ask about immersive, non-resort Maui.
Insider Tips
Mama’s Fish House reservations are genuinely difficult to get year-round — not just in peak season. Book as far in advance as possible. Bar seating is sometimes available for walk-ins. Lunch reservations are slightly easier to secure than dinner.
Pā‘ia is the last fuel stop before Hāna — and the last reliable grocery point for 52 miles. Clients driving the Road to Hāna should stop here for gas, water, and food before continuing east. Do not skip this.
The Ho‘okipa overlook is off the highway on the mauka side just past the beach access road. The view from above is more dramatic than from beach level, and mid-to-late afternoon is the best time to catch windsurfers fully in action.
Sea turtles at Ho‘okipa typically come ashore between 3 and 5 PM. When turtles are in the haul-out zone, that section of beach is closed. Brief clients: do not approach, do not touch, and do not let children run toward them.
The North Shore is primarily a watching coast, not a swimming coast. The same trade wind conditions that produce world-class windsurfing make beach swimming inadvisable most afternoons. Set accurate expectations for clients who imagine a swim day here.
Hāiku cottage rentals typically run on longer minimum stays and are listed through VRBO or local property managers. For clients who want a week or more in a lush, unhurried setting, Hāiku is worth recommending as an alternative to a resort.

East Maui
Road to Hāna
The Road to Hāna is 52 miles of two-lane highway through the most botanically dense corridor on the island. It takes two to three hours to drive without stops — which means nobody actually does it that way. The waterfalls appear unexpectedly. The bamboo forests close out the sky. The one-lane bridges require patience and road courtesy. The point is not arrival. The point is the road itself, and the discipline of actually slowing down inside it.
Stops & Areas
Twin Falls — Mile Marker 2
The first decision
The first significant waterfall stop after leaving Pā‘ia — an easy, well-marked trail accessible to most fitness levels. Worth the 30-minute detour. It sets the pace and establishes that this drive is not a race. There is a fruit stand at the entrance. Get something from it.
Ke‘anae Peninsula
Halfway, and worth pausing
About halfway to Hāna, the peninsula juts out into open ocean — a flat lava shelf covered in taro patches, with a tiny village and a coral and lava rock church built in the 1860s. Aunty Sandy’s Banana Bread, a small roadside stand at the junction, is one of the most consistently mentioned stops on the entire drive. Get there before they sell out. The overlook above the peninsula reveals a coastline that looks nothing like resort Maui.
Wai‘ānapanapa State Park
Black sand, lava arches, sea cave
A volcanic black sand beach enclosed by lava cliffs, with blowhole arches and a freshwater sea cave with local legend attached. One of the most dramatic coastal landscapes on the island. Advance reservations are required for both parking and beach access — book through the Hawaii state park reservation system well before arrival. Enforcement is real and the spots fill up. This is not a casual pull-off.

Hāna Town
Small, quiet, and the whole point
Hāna has a gas station, a grocery store, a small bay with a black sand beach, and the Hotel Hāna-Maui — a minimalist, low-key property unlike any other resort on the island. The town has roughly 2,000 residents and no traffic lights. It rewards clients who overnight. One night in Hāna changes the entire trip — instead of rushing the 52 miles back in the dark, you wake up to open sky, walk to the bay, and understand why the drive was worth it.
Kīpahulu & Ohe‘o Gulch
South of Hāna, inside the national park
The southern section of Haleakalā National Park extends to the coast here. Ohe‘o Gulch — known in tourism marketing as the Seven Sacred Pools, a name with no traditional Hawaiian basis — is a series of tiered freshwater pools stepping toward the ocean. The Pīpīwai Trail begins here: roughly two miles each way through a cathedral bamboo forest to Waimoku Falls, which drops approximately 400 feet. The bamboo is otherworldly. Budget three to four hours for the round trip. Clients who make it this far and skip the trail miss the best part of the day.
Insider Tips
Drive the Road to Hāna eastbound in the morning. Traffic is lighter, the light is better for waterfalls, and you beat the tour vans to the major stops. Starting from Pā‘ia by 7 or 8 AM puts you well ahead of the mid-morning buildup.
Wai‘ānapanapa reservations are time-slot specific for parking. Without one, clients will be turned away at the entrance. Book this before anything else on the Hāna itinerary.
The drive back from Hāna in the dark is not a good experience — the same 52 miles of narrow road with no center line in places and significantly more stress after dark. An overnight in Hāna is always the better option when budget allows.
Ke‘anae banana bread sells out. Aunty Sandy’s is a small roadside operation — when it’s gone, it’s gone. Arriving after noon on busy days means potentially missing it.
The Kīpahulu section of Haleakalā National Park is separate from the summit. A single park pass covers both, but the two sections do not connect by road. Driving from Kīpahulu to the summit requires backtracking all the way through Hāna and ascending via a completely separate highway. Clients who plan both in one day need to understand this geometry.
Hāna’s gas station operates on limited hours — not 24-hour. Clients continuing south to Kīpahulu should fill up in Hāna before going. There is no fuel south of Hāna town.
Cell service disappears along most of the Road to Hāna, particularly between the midpoint and Hāna town. Recommend that clients download offline maps before leaving Pā‘ia. Signal returns in Hāna.
The Pīpīwai Trail is muddy year-round. Closed-toe shoes or hiking sandals with grip are essential. Clients in flip flops will be unhappy before they reach the bamboo.

The Beach Right in Front
Kāʻanapali Beach ~3 miles of golden sand
You’re on one of the most celebrated beaches in Hawaiʻi — a long curve of golden sand with views across the channel to Lanaʻi and Molokaʻi. Walk out of your room, past the pool, and you’re on it. Generally good for swimming, with lifeguards typically present, though always read the daily conditions. Mornings are best for snorkeling — afternoon trade winds add chop and cut visibility.
Walkable from the Hotel
Black Rock (Puʻu Kekaʻa) ~5–10 min walk north
The lava promontory at the north end of the beach, just past the Sheraton. Two things happen here. By day it’s the area’s best easy snorkel — clear water, reef fish, frequent green sea turtles right off the rocks (best in the calmer morning). At sunset it becomes ceremony ground: Puʻu Kekaʻa is a sacred leina, a portal where Hawaiians believed souls leapt to the ancestral realm. Each evening a cliff diver lights the torches along the rock, blows the conch to the four directions, and dives — honoring Chief Kahekili, who proved his courage here. It lasts about ten minutes and is quietly moving. Watch from the beach; the jump itself is for experienced divers only.
Whalers Village ~10 min walk
The open-air oceanfront shopping and dining center on Kāʻanapali Parkway — around 90 shops and restaurants, from local Hawaiian stores to familiar names, plus a whaling-history museum. Beachfront restaurants here (Hula Grill, Leilani’s, Monkey Pod) are reliable sunset-dinner picks. Parking: up to 3 hours free with validation, paid beyond that — but you can simply walk the beach path from the hotel.
Kāʻanapali Beach Walk Paved beachfront path
A flat, paved path runs along the sand connecting the string of Kāʻanapali resorts, Whalers Village, and Black Rock. It’s the easiest way to get anywhere along the beach without driving — stroll it for dinner, sunset, or a morning walk. Resort beach showers and shop restrooms along the way are generally accessible to the public.
Beachfront Dining Nearby 5–15 min walk
Beyond your own property and Whalers Village, the walkable strip includes Duke’s Beach House (north toward Honua Kai) and the resort restaurants along the path. For a no-driving dinner with your toes near the sand, the Kāʻanapali stretch has more options within a 15-minute walk than most Maui addresses.
Insider Tips
Do the lei kukui ceremony at checkout — it’s easy to rush past on departure morning, but it’s the most meaningful two minutes of the stay.
Snorkel Black Rock early. Get in the water before the wind picks up (generally before ~11 AM) for the clearest visibility and the best turtle odds.
Walk, don’t drive, for sunset dinner. Front Street and Whalers Village parking is a hassle; the beach walk gets you there on foot in 10 minutes.
Catch the cliff-dive ceremony at the Black Rock end — arrive 15–20 minutes before sunset to get a spot on the sand.
Reef-safe sunscreen only. Hawaiʻi bans chemical sunscreens that harm coral — mineral (zinc oxide / titanium dioxide) is the rule.
No gym — the beach walk doubles as the morning workout; it’s flat and scenic in either direction.
Check the daily culture schedule at the lobby on arrival so you don’t miss the class you most want — hula and ʻukulele fill up.
Driving distances: Kahului Airport (OGG) ~45 min, Lahaina town ~10 min, the Road to Hāna trailhead is a full cross-island day from here — plan an early start.

Before you go — read this

Flash floods are real. Streams rise and turn brown in minutes even when it’s sunny where you stand — if the water clouds or you hear a roar upstream, leave immediately. No legal parking means no stop: most Hāna Highway falls have no lot, and pulling onto narrow shoulders is illegal, blocks emergency vehicles, and risks towing. Respect private property — much of this land is private or East Maui Irrigation infrastructure; if no sign permits entry, don’t enter.

The Road to Hāna is Maui’s waterfall corridor — but the honest truth most guides skip is that only a handful of falls have legal parking, and many famous ones are drive-by glimpses with nowhere safe to stop. These are sorted exactly that way: managed stops you can actually pull into, drive-by views to enjoy from the car, and a couple reachable only by helicopter or a serious hike. Go in the morning, before the rain and the crowds.

Maui Beaches
Filter by region
Maui beaches are not all the same. South Maui (Kīhei through Wailea) is calm year-round — safe for families, easy entries, excellent water clarity. West Maui has spectacular settings but stronger shore break. North Shore is world-class in summer and dangerous in winter. Big Beach at Mākena looks inviting and has injured more tourists than any other beach on the island — the shore break is violent and unpredictable. Know where you’re going before you get in the water.
Beach Safety & Tips
South Maui is the safest year-round: The Kāma'ole beaches, Keawakapu, Ulua, and the Wailea stretch have the calmest conditions on the island. Best choice for families and young children.
Big Beach (Mākena) warning: The shore break here is notoriously violent. People are injured every week. The beach looks calm — it is not. Observe the waves for several minutes before entering.
North Shore seasonality: Hōkūpa'a, Baldwin, and Lanes are excellent in summer. Winter swells make them unsafe for swimming. Always check surf conditions.
Red Sand Beach (Kaihalulu): The cliff walk to get there is short but genuinely slippery. Closed-toe shoes and caution required — people fall every season.
Wailea beach parking fills by 9 AM on busy days. Arrive early or park at one beach and walk the coastal path to others.
No lifeguards at most Maui beaches. Self-assessment is essential. If you see warning flags or signs — read them.
Never turn your back on the ocean — even on calm days. Rogue sets happen on every beach.

Respect
People live here — drive courteously
Mile markers lie. They reset and overlap along the highway — never rely on them for safety. The rule that keeps you safe: stop only where there is a real parking area. If a site has no marked lot or wide gravel turnout that fits your whole vehicle off the road, it is a view-from-the-moving-car landmark. Don’t reverse, U-turn, or park on the shoulder.

GatewayHwy 36

Pāʻia (the Hawaiian word means ‘noisy’) is Maui’s bohemian North Shore gateway — pastel plantation storefronts, surf shops, art galleries, and unhurried hippie charm. Once a quiet sugar plantation town, Pāʻia woke up in the ’70s and ’80s when hippies and windsurfers arrived — the bohemian-windsurf identity it’s still known for. Well worth a wander on the way back. On a Hāna morning, though, treat it as a required staging stop, not a maybe.

  • Fill the gas tank. The next reliable fuel is Hāna, hours away and pricey.
  • Grab food & water. Mana Foods for provisions; Pāʻia Fish Market or a bakery for the road.
  • Use the restroom and download offline maps — cell service drops out fast past town.
  • Leave early (on the road by 7–8 AM) to beat both the tour vans and the afternoon rain.

Hoʻokipa Beach Lookout
~MM 9ManagedHwy 36

The world’s windsurfing capital — sails and kites stitch the bay on windy afternoons, and green sea turtles haul out on the sand to bask late in the day. A paved overlook with parking sits just past the beach on the makai (ocean) side. Quick stop, big payoff.

2
Twin Falls
~MM 2ManagedWaterfall

The first waterfall and the easiest, with three features in the area — Twin Falls itself, a Rope Swing, and the Caves. On private land at Wailele Farm, with a paid lot and a farm stand at the gate — the only safe, legal way in. No roadside parking allowed. A flat 10–15 minute walk reaches the lower falls and pool. Arrive before 9 AM; both the lot and the trail fill quickly. Heads up: the highway changes from Route 36 to Route 360 around here and the mile markers reset to 0.

Flash-flood note: never enter the streambed or pool if water is brown, rising, or rain is falling in the mountains above — it can surge in seconds.

Rainbow Eucalyptus Grove
~MM 7View Only

Trees whose bark peels into streaks of green, orange, and violet, like they’ve been painted. They appear in clusters along the road — there is no parking and no single ‘stop.’ Admire them from the moving vehicle; do not pull onto the shoulder.

Waikamoi Ridge Trail
~MM 9–10Pull-offShort Hike

A short forest loop through towering bamboo, eucalyptus, and ginger — a good leg-stretch and a taste of the rainforest. Parking is a small, informal gravel turnout; only stop if your whole vehicle fits clear of the lane. Trail can be muddy and slick.

Garden of Eden Arboretum
~MM 10ManagedGarden

A private botanical garden with paid entry and a real parking lot — a calm, legal alternative to fighting for roadside space. Kaumahina State Wayside
~MM 12ManagedOverlook

A public state park with a marked lot, restrooms, and picnic tables — one of the few places you can legally pull off and breathe. The overlook frames the rugged Keʻanae coastline and the cliff valleys where waterfalls thread down. A smart reset point on the long middle stretch.

Keʻanae Arboretum
~MM 16Pull-offGarden

A free county arboretum with shaded walking paths through rainbow eucalyptus, taro, and tropical plantings. A small, often-tight parking area; only stop if you fit fully off the road. A quiet contrast to the busier paid garden up the hill.

Keʻanae Peninsula
~MM 16.5 turnoffPull-off

Turn off and drop down to a flat tongue of black lava where the surf explodes against the rocks. Here you’ll find Lanakila ʻIhiʻihi o Iēhova church (1860, built of coral and stone), working taro patches, and a tight-knit Hawaiian community — this is a lived-in place, not a photo set, so tread lightly. Aunty Sandy’s banana bread, sold warm from a roadside window here, is legendary — same family, 40+ years. Closed Sundays. Limited street parking.

Keʻanae Valley Overlook
~MM 17Pull-offOverlook

A highway pullout looking up Keʻanae Valley toward the rainforest interior, where waterfalls appear after rain. A brief, scenic pause if a safe turnout is open.

11
Halfway to Hāna Stand
~MM 17–18Pull-offFood

The famous little stand marking the rough midpoint to Hāna town — banana bread, shave ice, cold drinks. A small gravel pull-off; bring cash. A good morale (and bathroom) checkpoint before the second half. Fair warning: if you’re continuing past Hāna to Kīpahulu, you’re actually only about ⅓ of the way there from here — pace accordingly.

Upper Waikani Falls (Three Bears)
~MM 19View OnlyWaterfall

Three cascades side by side dropping into one pool — one of the most photographed falls on the drive, framed by the bridge. There is no official parking; visitors crowd narrow shoulders and blind curves, which blocks traffic and draws fines. Slow and admire from the bridge; stop only if a genuinely safe, legal turnout is open.

Flash-flood note: never enter the streambed or pool if water is brown, rising, or rain is falling in the mountains above — it can surge in seconds.

13
Wailua Valley State Wayside
ManagedOverlook

A marked state wayside with an official lot and a short stairway to an overlook of Wailua village, its taro fields, and the coast. Legal, safe, and easy to miss — watch for the brown state-park sign.

14
Puaʻa Kaʻa State Wayside
~MM 22.5ManagedWaterfall

One of the only legal waterfall stops on the highway. A state wayside with a marked lot, restrooms, and picnic tables; a short paved path leads to small falls and pools right beside the parking — no scramble, no trespassing, no traffic risk. The single best place to safely see moving water in this stretch. Can get crowded with tour vans midday — if it’s slammed, push 10 minutes ahead to Nahiku Landing for a similar (and usually quieter) picnic spot.

Flash-flood note: never enter the streambed or pool if water is brown, rising, or rain is falling in the mountains above — it can surge in seconds.

Hanawī Falls
~MM 24View OnlyWaterfall

A tall, layered fall visible from the bridge. The shoulders are tight and the curve limits sightlines, so stepping out here is genuinely risky. Admire from the car; do not park unless a clear, fully-off-road turnout is open.

Makapipi Falls
~MM 25View OnlyWaterfall

Unusual in that you look straight down on it from the bridge rather than up. No real parking and dangerously narrow edges — a quick glance from the bridge is the safe play. Keep moving if traffic is behind you.

17
Nahiku & Coconut Glen’s
Pull-offFood

A quiet former rubber-plantation village down a side road, and along the highway, Coconut Glen’s — small-batch vegan coconut-milk ice cream served in a coconut shell from a roadside stand. Just past Glen’s is the Nahiku Marketplace, a small cluster of food stands (fish tacos, smoothies, BBQ) — a useful food cluster between Puaʻa Kaʻa and Hāna. Small pull-offs; cash helps.

18
Kahanu Garden & Piʻilanihale Heiau
ʻUlaʻino RdManagedCultural

Down ʻUlaʻino Road near Hāna, a National Tropical Botanical Garden built around Piʻilanihale Heiau — the largest ancient temple in all of Polynesia, completed around 1570 A.D. and once one of the most sacred sites in all of Hawaiʻi. Paid entry and parking. A serious cultural cornerstone, not a quick selfie stop; allow real time and visit with respect.

Waiʻānapanapa State Park
Black SandReservation

The famous jet-black sand beach, sea caves, blowholes, and a walkable lava tube — the one sanctioned cave on the route. Out-of-state visitors must reserve parking and entry in advance — there is no walk-up for non-residents, and no legal parking outside the lots. Surf is powerful; swim only when calm and posted safe.

Hāna Town
ManagedServices

The destination, and a slow, gracious town that rewards lingering. Hasegawa General Store (a Hāna institution since 1910 that sells nearly everything), Hāna Bay’s calm swimming, and a handful of food trucks and plate-lunch spots. Fuel here is limited-hours and expensive — you topped off in Pāʻia for this reason.

21
Kōkī & Hāmoa Beaches
Haneoʻo RdPull-offBeach

Just past Hāna on the Haneoʻo Road loop. Kōkī is a wild red-sand beach beneath the Ka Iwi o Pele (‘the bones of Pele’) cinder cone — dramatic, but strong currents make it for looking, not swimming. Just inland is the ancient Haneoʻo fishpond, still maintained and functional today — a remarkable feat of pre-contact Hawaiian engineering. Hāmoa, a short way on, is the crescent of gray sand James Michener called one of the most beautiful in the Pacific — better for a careful swim or bodysurf. Decent snorkeling on the far-left side when waves aren’t too strong. Roadside parking is limited.

22
Wailua Falls
Hwy 31View OnlyWaterfall

A dramatic 80-foot roadside fall on the stretch past Hāna toward Kīpahulu — arguably the most photogenic on the island. Best viewed from the bridge; the standard move is to park to the left past the bridge and walk back. A small trail leads to the base. The catch is a tiny pull-off that fills instantly and pushes drivers onto a blind shoulder — stop only if a safe, fully-off-road space is open; otherwise keep driving.

Flash-flood note: never enter the streambed or pool if water is brown, rising, or rain is falling in the mountains above — it can surge in seconds.

23
Kīpahulu — Pools of ʻOheʻo & Pīpīwai Trail
Haleakalā NPFee

The grand finale, in the Kīpahulu District of Haleakalā National Park — park at the Kīpahulu Visitor Center (entry fee or park pass). The Pools of ʻOheʻo step down to the sea in tiers (swimming is frequently closed for flood risk — check the posted status). The Pīpīwai Trail climbs 4 miles round-trip with 650 ft of elevation gain (~2 hours) past 185-foot Makahiku Falls and through a roaring bamboo forest to 400-foot Waimoku Falls — one of the great hikes in Hawaiʻi. Allow 3–4 hours if you hike it.

Flash-flood note: never enter the streambed or pool if water is brown, rising, or rain is falling in the mountains above — it can surge in seconds.

Driving Guide
Kahekili Highway
West Maui Drive
The wild northwest face of Maui — the island’s other great drive. The Kahekili Highway loop runs from Kapalua around the top of the island to Wailuku, threading cliff-edge one-lane sections, sacred Hawaiian landmarks, a tiny living village, and some of the most dramatic ocean views in Hawai‘i. It’s shorter than Road to Hāna (~38 miles loop) but more genuinely demanding to drive. Drive it clockwise from Kapalua — this keeps you on the inside (uphill) lane, away from the cliff edge. Counterclockwise from Wailuku puts you on the outside drop, which locals don’t recommend for visitors. Stops below run in driving order, Kapalua to Wailuku.

Kapalua · Pre-Drive
GatewayHwy 30

Kapalua and Kā‘anapali are your last reliable services before the loop. Treat this as a required staging stop, not optional.

  • Fill the gas tank. The next reliable pump is in Wailuku, three-plus hours away even without stops.
  • Grab food & water. No shops on the highway.
  • Use the restroom. Public restrooms are scarce on the loop.
  • Check your rental contract. Many companies forbid Kahekili Hwy — doing it anyway can void coverage.
  • Download offline maps. Cell drops out past Honolua.
20 min for fuel, food & restrooms
1
D.T. Fleming Beach Park
~MM 32ManagedBeach

A wide, lifeguarded beach park just past the Ritz-Carlton with a real parking lot, restrooms, and showers — an optional warm-up stop and the last managed beach before things get wild. Body-boarding can be good when the swell isn’t too big. Spend 30 minutes here or skip and push on.

15–45 min
2
Honolua Bay
~MM 32–33Pull-offSnorkel/Surf

A protected marine reserve and one of Maui’s premier snorkel coves in calm conditions, plus a world-class surf break in winter. From the highway pull-off, a moss-canopied jungle path winds five minutes down to the rocky bay (also covered in Photo Spots and Hiking). Parking is roadside — arrive early. No facilities. Winter surf is for experienced surfers only; stay on shore as a spectator.

3
Nakalele Blowhole + Acid War Zone
~MM 38.5Pull-off⚠ Deadly

A geyser of seawater firing 50–100 feet through a lava tube on the wild northwest coast, framed by an eroded otherworldly landscape locals call the Acid War Zone. Mini hike from the main lot at MM 38.5, or a longer 1.3-mile Acid War Zone Trail from the smaller lot at MM 38.

People have been killed here, pulled into the blowhole by retreating water. Stay well back from the edge; never turn your back to the ocean; the rocks are extremely slippery from spray. The famous Heart-Shaped Rock has reportedly eroded — verify before promising it to anyone.

4
Ohai Loop Trail
~MM 40–41ManagedEasy Hike

One of the best short hikes on Maui’s north coast — a 1.2-mile loop above the cliffs through native ‘ohai shrubs with panoramic ocean views the whole way. Easy enough for most fitness levels (athletic shoes fine), about 30 minutes. Whale views Dec–Apr. No shade — bring water and sun protection.

30–45 min
5
Olivine Pools & Pohaku Kani (Bellstone)
Pull-off⚠ DeadlySacred

Saltwater tide pools set in a black lava bench below the cliffs — mirror-still when calm, lethal when not. People have been swept off these rocks by surprise waves and killed. Photograph from the cliff above; only descend on glass-flat ocean days, and never turn your back to the sea.

Right by the parking area is Pohaku Kani, the Bellstone — an ancient Hawaiian sacred stone said to ring like a bell when struck in the right spot. One of the few legal cultural sites on this drive. Treat with respect: not a souvenir.

6
Kahakuloa Head Viewpoint
View OnlyLandmark

The dramatic 636-foot dome (Pu‘u Koa‘e) rising from the sea cliffs above Kahakuloa Village — one of the most striking landmarks on the West Maui coast. Marked overlook near MM 15 with views down into the village below and across to the head itself. The road tightens dramatically from here as it descends into the valley — downshift, take it slow.

7
Kahakuloa Village
~MM 14–15Pull-offCash Only

A tiny, time-stilled Hawaiian village of about 100 residents folded into a deep green valley — the cultural and emotional heart of this drive and the most-loved refreshment stop. The legends:

  • Julia’s Banana Bread — a green wooden shack; homemade bread, fruit, chilled coconut water.
  • Lorraine’s Banana Bread & Shave Ice — if she’s open, stop.
  • Bruddah Chic — smoothies and a‘cai bowls with a killer view of the head.
  • Karen Lei’s Gallery and Bruce Turnbull Studio & Sculpture Garden — worth a stop for art.

Cash only. No sit-down spots. No public restrooms. Drive slowly, park courteously off the lane, don’t photograph homes or residents. This is a living community, not a tourist set.

30–60 min
8
Ranch Country
Drive-Through

After Kahakuloa, the road climbs out of the valley into wide pastoral ranch country — rolling green pastures, grazing cattle, sweeping views down to the coast. No designated stops, just enjoy the contrast with the cliffs you just drove. The road begins to widen and feel less tense from here on.

9
Waiheʻe Ridge Trailhead
~MM 7ManagedHike

The trailhead for one of Maui’s best moderate hikes — a 5-mile ridge climb into the clouds with panoramic views across the West Maui Mountains (also covered in Hiking). Quick stop for a viewpoint from the lower lot, or a half-day commitment if you hike it. Gate 7 AM–7 PM. Off the highway at Maluhia Road, across from Mendes Ranch.

10
Waiheʻe Coastal Dunes & Beach Park
ManagedBeach

A calm, often-empty beach and protected wetland reserve at the end of the wild stretch — a Hawaiian Islands Land Trust property with restored coastal dunes, a small beach, and shorebird habitat. Public restrooms (one of the few on the loop). A peaceful, uncrowded place to close out the dramatic part of the drive.

11
Wailuku Town · End of Drive
ServicesFood & Coffee

Wailuku is the historic county seat — old plantation buildings, Market Street’s shops and restaurants, and the natural endpoint of the loop. Stop for lunch, grab a coffee, hit a restroom. From here, ‘Īao Valley is a 10-minute side trip up the road for an easy add-on — reservation required for out-of-state visitors, and check current closure status (safety work through June 26, 2026). Return to West Maui via Hwy 30 along the south coast.

Kahului · Pre-Drive
StartHwy 32 then 30

Start full and stocked. Kahului has Costco, Safeway, and quick coffee — last big-box stops before the loop. Fill the tank, grab water and snacks, use the restroom. Allow a full day, 8+ hours from start to back at the hotel.

1
Old Bailey House Museum
WailukuManagedCultural

A modest white-walled missionary home from 1833 now serving as the Maui Historical Society’s museum — one of the oldest Western structures on the island, set against the West Maui mountains. The collection includes Hawaiian artifacts (kapa, fishing tools, calabashes), missionary-era furnishings, and paintings by Edward Bailey himself. A thoughtful primer before driving up into ‘Īao Valley just up the road. Open Mon–Sat 10 AM–4 PM, admission ~$10.

30–45 min
2
‘Īao Valley State Monument
ReservationSacred Site⚠ Closed thru 6/26

The sacred green amphitheater of Central Maui — 1,200-foot Kūka‘emoku (the ‘Īao Needle) towering above a stream-cut valley where Kamehameha won the bloody 1790 Battle of Kepaniwai. Short paved walks to the lookout; reservation required for out-of-state visitors at gostateparks.hawaii.gov. Currently closed for safety improvements through June 26, 2026 — verify before going.

Maui Tropical Plantation
WaikapūManagedFarm Tour

A working tropical farm and outdoor garden at the foot of the West Maui Mountains. Tram tours through fields of papaya, coffee, mac nut, and exotic flowers; on-site Mill House restaurant (excellent farm-driven menu); Hawaiian Coffee Roasters and Maui Tropicals retail. Free entry to the grounds; tram and tours are paid. Also covered in Farms.

Maui Ocean Center
~MM 5 Hwy 30ManagedIndoor

Hawai‘i’s largest aquarium and one of the most visitor-friendly stops on Maui — the only place to see Hawaiian reef fish and pelagic species (sharks, sea turtles, rays) up close without getting in the water. Includes a giant Open Ocean tunnel, the immersive humpback whale theater (in season), and excellent interpretive signage on Hawaiian ocean culture. Indoor and air-conditioned — great rainy-day stop. Tickets ~$45 adult. Also covered in Unique Experiences.

Olowalu Petroglyphs
~MM 15Pull-offCultural

A small panel of pre-contact Hawaiian rock carvings (pu‘u kilea) on a basalt cliff face behind the Olowalu General Store — not the largest petroglyph site in Hawai‘i but accessible and culturally significant. Treat with respect: no touching, no chalk, no climbing. The drive in passes through Olowalu’s tragic history (the 1790 attack on the Fair American and its bloody retaliation under Captain Simon Metcalfe). Also covered in Historical Sights.

Leoda’s Kitchen & Pie Shop

A roadside legend — small bakery and lunch counter known across Maui for pies (the banana cream, chocolate haupia, and macadamia nut chocolate are reliably excellent). Lāhainā · A Living Recovery
~MM 22View OnlyCultural

Once Maui’s cultural and shopping heart, historic Front Street was largely destroyed in the August 2023 wildfire. As of 2026, the area remains primarily closed to commercial activity while recovery and rebuilding plans move forward. Front Street is not currently a destination — drive past with respect for what was lost. The Cannery Mall, Lahaina Gateway, and small operators on the north end are open and recovering. Spend generously when you stop there. See the Culture guide for more on Lāhainā’s return.

Kā‘anapali Beach
ManagedBeachSnorkel

Three miles of golden sand fronting the Kā‘anapali resorts — the West Side’s main resort beach and the easiest place for a long beach walk on Maui. Lunch at Whaler’s Village (covered in Dining) is a natural pause. Also covered in Beaches.

A short flat walk across a coastal lava bench to the famous Dragon’s Teeth at Makaluapuna Point — jagged spires of black lava sculpted by wind and surf into shapes that look like dragon mouths. Altitude is real. 10,023 ft is genuine altitude — you may feel lightheaded, short of breath, or get a mild headache. Acclimate for 30+ minutes in Pukalani on the way up. Do not drive up within 24 hours of scuba diving (decompression risk). Pregnancy, heart conditions, and severe asthma should consult a doctor.

Stargazing tips. Haleakalā has some of the darkest skies in the world. Bring a red flashlight (white light wrecks night vision for everyone else), warm layers and a blanket, and a reclining beach chair if you have one. The Milky Way is brightest April–September. Hiking & Adventures
Filter by region
Maui’s trails range from paved boardwalks at the resorts to a 12-mile descent into a volcanic crater above the clouds. The lineup below is organized by region with a difficulty tag on every entry, so you can match the hike to the day. A few are genuinely dangerous if you ignore the safety notes — sun, surf, altitude, flash floods, sharp lava — so the tips below are written for staying out of trouble, not for scenery.
Current Conditions & Resources
Maui’s weather can shift fast. In March 2026, severe Kona storms dropped record rainfall (49+ inches at the Haleakalā summit in five days), closing both park districts and damaging roads across the island. Both districts reopened by the end of March, but smaller closures and lingering road repairs continue. Always check current conditions before any hike, and honor posted closures — they exist for safety and for protecting land that is still recovering. As a visitor, that’s your kuleana (responsibility).

Before You Hike
Start early. Most Maui trails are best at dawn — afternoon brings heat, rain, or crowds.
Real shoes. Closed-toe trail shoes or hikers, not sandals. Maui lava is sharp; windward mud is slick.
Bring more water than you think. 2+ liters per person, 3+ on exposed or high-elevation hikes.
Layers for elevation. The Haleakalā summit (10,023 ft) and Polipoli run cold and often wet at any time of year — even in August.
Reservations matter. ‘Īao Valley, Wai‘ānapanapa, and Haleakalā sunrise all require advance booking. Note: ‘Īao Valley is closed for safety work through June 26, 2026.
Don’t park on highway shoulders. Trailhead break-ins are common; lock nothing visible in the car.
Flash floods are real. Near streams (Pīpīwai, Twin Falls, Kuloa Point), if the water turns brown or rises, leave the area immediately.
Stay on the trail. Off-trail ‘shortcuts’ often cross sacred sites, private land, or unstable terrain.

Maui Gardens
Filter by region
Maui's gardens climb from steamy Hāna rainforest to the cool, misty slopes of Upcountry Kula — a reflection of the island's wild range of microclimates. Some are manicured showpieces of protea and orchids; others are rustic, free, self-guided tangles of taro and ginger along the Hāna Highway; and a few are living cultural sites preserving native and Polynesian-introduced plants. The Upcountry gardens run noticeably cooler and mistier than the coast, so bring a light layer.
Before You Go
Reservations & hours vary — Kahanu Garden (Maui's NTBG site, in Hāna) keeps limited weekday hours and the D.T. Fleming Arboretum is by arrangement only; the paid gardens (Garden of Eden, Kula Botanical) have set hours, while the Sacred Garden, Kepaniwai, and Hosmer Grove are free. Check ahead.
Mornings are best — Maui warms fast and afternoon clouds and showers roll in, especially Upcountry and along the Road to Hāna.
Closed-toe shoes recommended — paths at Keʻanae Arboretum and Hosmer Grove can be muddy, rooty, or uneven.
Reef-safe bug spray — bring it. Mosquitoes are real in the shaded East Maui and rainforest gardens.
Dress in layers Upcountry — Kula and Hosmer Grove (about 6,800 ft) run much cooler and mistier than the coast.
Allow more time than you think — once you're walking, you'll want another hour.

Coffee on Maui
Filter by region
Maui grows its own coffee — mostly on Upcountry farms and small operations around the island. But the best coffee experiences are the independent roasters and neighborhood cafes scattered from Kahului to Hāna. Paʻia alone has five worth knowing. The Upcountry sweep is quieter but genuinely good. And on the Road to Hāna, Paʻia is the last proper espresso stop for 52 miles — plan accordingly.
Insider Tips
Maui-grown beans are the real thing — the Kaʻanapali Estate (MauiGrown) on the West Side and small Kula farms produce them — but they're different from Kona, which is grown on the Big Island, and from imported blends. Ask which you're drinking.
“100% Hawaiian” or “100% Kona” on the menu is the tell — many cafés pour Kona or imported beans, while a handful (Akamai Coffee, Lava Java, Maui Coffee Roasters) actually roast on-island.
Local coffee costs more than mainland drip — worth it for a cup grown a few ridges away rather than a continent away.

Snorkeling on Maui
Filter by region
Maui has some of the best shore snorkeling in Hawaii — healthy reefs, exceptional turtle density, and a handful of spots that rival anything in the Pacific. The West Shore has the most concentrated options (Honolua Bay, Olowalu, Black Rock, Kapalua Bay) but South Maui delivers the most consistent turtle encounters (Maluaka, Mākena Landing, Ulua). Go early — trade winds roughen conditions by early afternoon on most of the island.
Snorkeling Tips
Reef-safe sunscreen only — Hawaii state law, fines enforced. Oxybenzone and octinoxate are banned.
Never touch coral or turtles. Both are protected. Coral dies on contact and turtles are federally protected — 10-foot minimum distance required by law.
Go early. Morning is almost always calmer than afternoon. By noon, trade winds pick up and visibility drops at exposed sites.
Molokini Crater is the premier boat snorkel — a submerged volcanic caldera 2.5 miles offshore with visibility exceeding 150 feet on good days. Book a morning charter from Mā‘alaea Harbor.
Turtle Town is a loose term for the Mākena/Ulua area — sea turtles rest and graze on the reef here at reliably higher density than anywhere else on the South Shore.
Gear: Most West Maui dive shops rent snorkel sets for $10–15/day. Prescription masks are available at larger outfitters if needed.
Honolua Bay is seasonal: Best snorkeling is April–October when it’s calm. Winter swells turn it into a surf break — beautiful to watch, not to swim.

Historical Sights
Filter by region
Maui holds some of the most significant historical and sacred sites in all of Hawaiʻi — ancient heiau (temples), kiʻi pōhaku (petroglyphs), royal grounds, and Lāhainā, once the royal capital of the Kingdom. Visiting them calls for pono (righteousness): stay on marked paths, never touch, climb, or move stones, keep a quiet and respectful demeanor, and mind any signs that restrict photography. These are not ruins — they are living, revered places.
Visiting With Pono
Do not touch. Never touch, climb, or move stones at a heiau or petroglyph site — these are sacred, still-revered religious sites, not ruins.
Stay on paths. Archaeological sites erode and damage easily; designated trails protect them for future generations.
Respect quiet. Keep a calm, respectful demeanor at heiau and burial sites.
Photography. Generally allowed, but mind any signs that prohibit it in sacred areas.
Many sites have no staff — the responsibility to visit respectfully falls to you.

Culture & Etiquette
Hawaii is not just a U.S. beach destination — it's a place with a living culture, a deep history, and a native language that's still spoken. Visitors who understand even a little about how things work here get treated differently. This is the short version of showing up right.
The Aloha Spirit
Aloha isn't just "hello" — it's a concept. It means love, compassion, presence, and the acknowledgment that we're all connected. When a Hawaiian says "aloha," they're saying something closer to "I see you." Take it seriously and it comes back to you.
The pace is slower than the mainland. People take their time. They talk to strangers. They don't rush through transactions. Match their rhythm — it's part of the experience.
Lāhainā — A Living Recovery
On August 8, 2023, a wind-driven wildfire roared through Lāhainā in a matter of hours and became the deadliest U.S. wildfire in more than a century. More than a hundred people were killed, thousands of homes and businesses burned, and an entire historic town — once the royal capital of the Hawaiian Kingdom — was reduced almost entirely to ash. The 1873 banyan tree survived, scarred and slowly recovering. The community survived too, scarred and slowly recovering. This is not history. People are still displaced. Families are still rebuilding. When you visit West Maui, you’re walking through a place that is still healing in real time.
Where Things Stand
The wider West Maui visitor corridor — Kā‘anapali, Honokōwai, Nāpili, and Kapalua — was not burned and is fully open. Tourism is the lifeblood of this side of the island, and locals genuinely need you here.
Parts of Lāhainā town are reopening, including stretches of Front Street and the harbor area. Rebuilding is slow but real — debris is cleared, permits are being issued, and a small but growing number of homes have been completed.
The historic core, Banyan Tree Park, and many of the destroyed cultural sites (the Old Lahaina Courthouse, Baldwin Home, Wo Hing Museum) remain closed or under restoration, with no firm reopening dates. Access shifts week to week — check the County’s MauiRecovers updates before going.
Visiting With Respect
Come. West Maui needs visitors. Staying away ‘out of respect’ hurts the people you’re trying to respect — tourism is roughly 70% of every dollar earned on the island.
Spend where it counts. Patronize Lāhainā businesses that have reopened. Tip generously. The people serving you are often working hard while still carrying enormous personal loss.
Obey the barriers. If something is fenced, signed, or marked closed, it is closed. Don’t enter the burn zone for photos. Don’t drive through restricted areas to ‘see it.’ This is not a tourist site.
No disaster photos. No selfies in front of ruined lots. No drone footage of burn areas. What you’d photograph is someone’s lost home, someone’s family land, or iwi kūpuna (ancestral remains) ground.
Don’t ask survivors about it. Servers, drivers, shop staff, guides — many lost family, friends, homes, or all three. If they bring it up, listen. If they don’t, don’t.
Use the right name. It is Lāhainā (lah-HEE-nah, with a long ā in both syllables) — not ‘the fire zone.’ Saying it correctly is its own small act of respect.
How to Help
The most widely pointed-to recovery fund is the Maui Strong Fund at the Hawai‘i Community Foundation (hawaiicommunityfoundation.org), which distributes to vetted local organizations on the ground. Beyond that, your tip at a reopened Lāhainā restaurant and the dollars you spend at a Lāhainā-based small business go directly to the families who need them most. For most visitors, that is the most useful thing you can do.
Taking Off Shoes
Always take off shoes before entering someone's home. Always. Even if they say it's fine.
Some vacation rentals ask the same — look for a shoe pile by the door.
Most hotels, restaurants, and shops are shoes-on. Use common sense.
Slip-on sandals (called "slippahs" here) are worn 90% of the time for this reason.
Tipping in Hawaii
Restaurants
18–22% standard. 20% is the baseline for good service. Servers here rely on tips.
Tour Guides (Na Pali boat, Waimea tours)
15–20% of the tour cost, or $10–20 per person for shorter excursions. Captains and guides work hard — tip generously if they made the day memorable.
Taxi / Uber / Shuttle Drivers
15–20% for taxis. For airport shuttle, $2–5 per bag is standard.
Hotel Housekeeping
$3–5 per day, left daily (not at the end) since different staff may rotate. A note of thanks helps.
Bellhops & Valet
$2–3 per bag for bellhops. $3–5 for valet when they bring the car.
Bartenders
$1–2 per drink, or 18–20% on a tab.

Being a Good Guest
Say "Hawaii" — not "the islands." Locals find the generalization grating.
Don't call yourself Hawaiian unless you're ethnically Native Hawaiian. "Local" or "from Hawaii" is the right word for residents who weren't born of Hawaiian blood.
Honu (green sea turtles): stay at least 10 ft back on land or in the water — 150 ft if a turtle is nesting. Never touch or feed them.
Hawaiian monk seals: stay 50 ft back, and 150 ft from mothers with pups. They’re critically endangered, and harassment carries serious federal fines.
Don't stack rocks on beaches or trails — it looks zen but disrupts native ecosystems and disrespects sacred sites.
At heiaus (sacred sites), don't climb, don't move stones, don't leave offerings without guidance from a Native Hawaiian.
Shaka (hand sign — thumb and pinky extended) is a friendly local greeting. Use it sincerely, not ironically.
Let locals merge in traffic — road rage is extremely un-local. A shaka after someone lets you in goes a long way.
Reef-safe sunscreen isn't optional — it's state law. Regular sunscreen kills coral and you can be fined.
One More Thing
The Hawaiian language was nearly erased by colonization. It's back now — official state language, taught in schools, visible on signs. When you use even one word correctly, you're participating in a cultural revival. Locals notice, and they appreciate it.

Photo Spots
Filter by region
Maui hands a photographer almost every landscape at once — alpine crater desert above the clouds, jungle waterfalls, black-lava coastline, and miles of west-facing sunset beach. The spots below are grouped by what you’re after, from golden-hour sand to dramatic blowholes to the rainbow eucalyptus of the Hāna Highway. Shoot the light, not the clock — the first hour after sunrise and the last hour before sunset do most of the work. And the island’s cardinal rule: only stop at marked pull-offs and lots, never on narrow shoulders (especially the Road to Hāna and Kahekili Highway).
Before You Shoot
Golden hour does the work — the first hour after sunrise and the last before sunset give the best light. Set up 30 minutes early.
Weather is a tool — overcast days make waterfalls, rainforest greens, and rainbow-eucalyptus bark richer, while beaches and sunsets want clear skies.
Only stop at marked pull-offs and lots — never the narrow shoulders of the Road to Hāna or Kahekili Highway. It’s dangerous and draws fines.
Flash floods are real — near waterfalls and streams, if the water turns brown or starts rising, leave immediately.
Respect the land — many viewpoints are sacred or on private property. Obey ‘kapu’ and ‘no trespassing’ signs and stay on the paths.
Mind the edges — coastal lava and blowhole spots have killed people. Keep well back from the surf and never turn your back to the ocean.

Farms & Tastings
Filter by region
Upcountry Maui is the island’s farming heartland — at 3,000–4,000 feet, the temperature drops, the soil changes, and the agriculture gets serious. Lavender, goat cheese, pineapple vodka, wine from a 19th-century ranch, and the best strawberries on the island all come from this corridor. Maui’s farm scene is more spread out than Kauai’s but just as rewarding. Most of the best experiences require a short reservation; a few are walk-up friendly.
Farm Visit Tips
Dress for Upcountry: When it’s 88°F at the beach, Kula can be 65°F with wind. Bring a layer for any Upcountry farm visit — especially morning or late afternoon.
Reserve Surfing Goat evening tours in advance — small group sizes and the milking-hour experience fill up. Daytime self-guided tours are walk-up friendly.
Ali‘i Kula Lavender is walk-up for self-guided visits but parking is limited. Arrive before 10 AM on busy days.
MauiWine tastings are walk-up; winery tours should be booked ahead. The property is one of the most scenic in all of Upcountry — budget time to linger.
Maui Tropical Plantation in Waikāpū (Central Maui) is the most accessible farm experience on the island — no mountain drive required. Good for clients who want the farm experience without the elevation.
TSA tip: Pack unopened jars, honey, and liquids from farm shops in checked luggage — they will be confiscated from carry-on.
Weekday mornings are the best time for Upcountry farm visits — tour vans arrive mid-morning on weekends and parking fills fast at the most popular stops.

Bars & Nightlife
Filter by region
Maui is a dinner-and-drinks island, not a club scene. Evenings are about a sunset cocktail on a lanai, live Hawaiian music, and a slow walk back along the beach. The Kā‘anapali Beach Walk at dusk is the natural gathering point on the west side. Monkeypod happy hour (3:30–5 PM) is a near-religious South Shore ritual. Fleetwood’s rooftop is what Lahaina does for a night out now. Most bars close by 10–11 PM.
After-Dark Tips
Last call statewide is 2 AM by Hawaii law. There is no after-hours scene anywhere on Maui.
Best sunset bar views: Fleetwood’s rooftop (Lahaina), Sea House happy hour (Nāpili Bay), Monkeypod at golden hour (Wailea & Kā‘anapali), Duke’s Beach House (Kā‘anapali North Beach).
Live music: Mulligan’s on the Blue (Wailea), Nalu’s South Shore (Kīhei), Monkeypod (both locations), Paia Bay Coffee & Bar (North Shore). Check current schedules — they shift.
Fleetwood’s rooftop is the post-fire Lahaina evening tradition — open to anyone, nightly live music, sunset views across the channel to Lāna‘i.
Monkeypod happy hour (3:30–5 PM, both Kā‘anapali and Wailea) is the best value cocktail window on the island. The passion fruit Mai Tai is the signature order.
Rideshare works well in Kā‘anapali, Wailea, Kīhei, and Lahaina. Coverage thins in Upcountry and East Maui at night. Plan transportation before heading out.
Dress code is resort-casual everywhere — flip-flops are fine at most bars. Resort fine dining bars (Ferraro’s, Morimoto Maui) expect a step up.

Shopping on Maui
Filter by region
Maui shopping has personality — it’s not one mall and a couple of beach shops. The South Shore brings open-air luxury (Tiffany, locally-curated boutiques, koi ponds at The Shops at Wailea). Upcountry Makawao is a paniolo town turned boho artist enclave where the cookies are oversized and the galleries are serious. Pā‘ia is the most browsable walking district on the island — bohemian, surfy, vintage. Kā‘anapali centers around Whaler’s Village on the beach. And Hāna has a 1910 general store that feels unchanged for a century. Listed below by region.
Shopping Smart on Maui
Authentic Maui souvenirs. Maui-grown coffee (not Kona — that’s the Big Island), Maui Gold pineapple, macadamia nut products, art from Maui Hands or the Maui Crafts Guild, and Ni‘ihau shell lei. Look for “Made in Hawai‘i” labels — many ‘Hawaiian’ souvenirs are mass-produced elsewhere.
Support Lāhainā’s recovery. When in West Maui, prioritize spending at Whaler’s Village, the Cannery Mall, and Lahaina Gateway. Those dollars go directly to small operators continuing to rebuild.
Cash for small stops. Most shops take cards. Bring small bills for roadside stands, farm stalls, and Kahakuloa Village (cash only).
Hawai‘i state tax is 4.166%. Lower than most mainland states. Some resort areas add a small additional district surcharge.
Shipping is common. Maui Hands ships, most galleries do too. Ask before buying anything large or fragile.
First Friday in Wailuku. Monthly evening street party on Market Street with food trucks, live music, and small vendors. Worth planning a Friday visit around if your dates align.

Farmers Markets
Filter by region
Maui’s farmers markets are where the island feeds itself — Kula vegetables grown in volcanic soil, fish landed that morning, fresh chilled coconuts cracked at the table, liliko‘i butter, mac nuts, banana bread by people whose families have made banana bread for 40 years. Markets run on different days in different regions; the lineup below is organized so you can match your travel days to the right market. The Saturday morning Upcountry Farmers Market in Pukalani is the flagship — if you only do one, do that.
Smart Tips for Maui Markets
Arrive early. 7–8 AM Saturdays gets the best selection. Coconuts, prepared foods, and fish sell out fast.
Bring cash and cards. Most vendors take cards now, but cash helps for small purchases and tips.
Bring reusable bags. Most markets are zero-waste-friendly; many sellers don’t provide plastic.
Try the unfamiliar. Breadfruit (‘ulu), rambutan, dragon fruit, apple bananas, liliko‘i — ask vendors what to try and how to eat it.
Look for ‘Made in Hawai‘i’ labels. Some ‘Maui’ souvenirs at markets are imported — the real local ones are clearly labeled and feel different in your hand.
The Maui Swap Meet in Kahului is far more than produce — the best one-stop if you’re hunting souvenirs, crafts, and gifts alongside food.

Festivals & Events

Maui’s festival calendar runs year-round — whale-season events and Pacific Whale Foundation gatherings through the winter, taro and ocean-culture festivals in spring, Kapalua wine and slack-key guitar in early summer, paniolo rodeo and resort fireworks in July, Festivals of Aloha through September and October, and a county fair plus the locally-made products show in the fall. Listed below by month so you can see what overlaps with your visit. Dates shift each year — always verify with the official site for each event closer to your trip. For week-to-week local events — smaller concerts, community gatherings, town meetings, surf comps — the Maui Now events calendar at mauinow.com/events is the best ongoing resource.
Festival Tips
The marquee ones: Kapalua Wine & Food Festival (late June), Maui Fair (October), and Made in Maui County Festival (early November) are the events visitors plan trips around. Buy tickets and book accommodations early.
For genuine local culture: East Maui Taro Festival (April, Hāna), Makawao Stampede (July, Upcountry), and Festivals of Aloha (Sept–Oct, multiple). These are where the island shows up for itself; visitors are welcomed, but it’s not built for you.
For music: the free Hawaiian Slack Key Guitar Festival (late June, MACC) is one of the most authentic experiences on the calendar.
Most are free or low-cost — community-funded festivals run on volunteers and donations. Bring cash for food vendors and tip generously.
Dates shift annually. Most festivals anchor to a weekend or month rather than a fixed date. Always verify with the official event site close to your trip.
Family-friendly throughout. Almost every Maui festival on this calendar welcomes keiki, and the bigger ones have dedicated children’s areas.
Whale-season events (Jan–Mar) double as ocean-conservation education — the Pacific Whale Foundation programming is uniformly excellent.
When Lāhainā Returns
Some of Maui’s most beloved annual traditions lived under the Banyan Tree in Lāhainā — the Halloween costume contest (sometimes called the ‘Mardi Gras of the Pacific’), the December Lighting of the Banyan Tree, the Banyan Tree Birthday in April, the Whale & Ocean Arts Festival, and Lāhainā Plantation Days. Banyan Court Park has remained closed since the August 2023 wildfire while arborists nurse the 1873 tree back to health, so these gatherings are on pause or have moved to smaller venues.
